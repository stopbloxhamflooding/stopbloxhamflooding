Why this matters

Flooding is not new to Bloxham — and there is no single cause.

The Oxfordshire County Council Section 19 investigation recorded up to 35 properties flooded internally during Storm Bert in November 2024. Community information suggests around 65 properties were affected more broadly, although a precise total is not known; at least five vehicles were also badly affected.

The formal investigation describes a combination of intense rainfall and infrastructure issues, including silted highway drainage, blockages, poorly maintained channels and relief culverts, sewer surcharging and capacity constraints. Responsibilities and actions therefore sit across several organisations as well as the community.

Historical evidence shows that Bloxham has been wrestling with flooding, water and drainage for more than 150 years. Our aim is to reduce flood risk and severity and improve resilience — not to suggest that any one intervention can prevent every flood.

Preparing for the future

Plan for heavier rainfall, whatever your view of climate change.

Flood resilience is about managing risk. Bloxham needs measures that work for today's storms while allowing for the possibility of more intense rainfall in the decades ahead.

Why build in resilience?

UK climate projections indicate an increase in the frequency and intensity of weather extremes, including heavier rainfall. The Met Office notes that heavy summer rainfall is projected to become more intense, with implications for the frequency and severity of surface-water flooding.

The Environment Agency already requires climate-change allowances for peak rainfall intensity and peak river flows when assessing and designing for future flood risk. In practical terms, that means drainage, storage and flood-resilience measures should not be designed only around what has happened in the past.

You do not have to agree about the causes of climate change to prepare for flood risk. Whatever your view, heavier storms can overwhelm drains, increase runoff and flood homes. Our focus is therefore practical: improve drainage, slow the flow and make Bloxham more resilient to the rainfall it may experience in future.

The Bloxham approach

Two areas. One objective.

Rain falling ON Bloxham needs to drain efficiently. Rain falling OUTSIDE the village in the wider catchment needs, where practical, to be slowed and temporarily stored.

Area 1

Rain falling ON Bloxham

Drain it efficiently.

  • Keep drains and gullies clear
  • Maintain drainage capacity
  • Identify restrictions and pinch points (places where water flow is restricted or backs up)
  • Allow rainfall on the village to get away
BLOXHAM

Give the village drainage system time and capacity to cope.

Drain first · delay the peak · release later

Area 2

Rain falling OUTSIDE Bloxham

Slow the flow.

  • Leaky woody dams
  • Field bunds and swales
  • Ponds, wetlands and temporary storage
  • Release water more gradually after the storm peak
NFM is part of the solution, not the whole solution. The 2026 Thames21 feasibility work, supported by Oxfordshire County Council, identified about 3,535 m³ of potential temporary storage across the proposals studied. Its modelling estimated a runoff reduction of around 1.3% for an event similar to November 2024 and around 3.5% for an average significant storm. That is why Bloxham needs both effective village drainage and measures that slow some water arriving from upstream.

During heavy rain

Avoid driving through floodwater wherever possible. If you do have to drive through shallow floodwater, proceed extremely slowly: vehicles can create bow waves that push water into nearby homes and businesses and make flooding worse. Consider delaying non-essential use of washing machines and dishwashers while the drainage system is under exceptional pressure.

After a flood: report it to OCC

After a flood, flooding should be reported to Oxfordshire County Council as the Lead Local Flood Authority so it can carry out a flood investigation where appropriate.

Hold back rain at home

Water butts capture roof runoff for later use. Permeable surfaces, planting and other measures that retain rainfall can also slow the rate at which water reaches drains.

Working together

Working with landowners, not against them.

Much of the potential Natural Flood Management work is on privately owned farmland. Participation is voluntary and success depends on practical cooperation with the people who own and farm the surrounding land.

A shared infrastructure problem

NFM is one part of a much wider response. The Section 19 investigation identifies actions and responsibilities involving Oxfordshire County Council, Thames Water, Cherwell District Council, the Environment Agency, the Parish Council, residents and landowners. It would be misleading to suggest that runoff from farmland is the sole cause of Bloxham's flooding, or that farmers alone can solve it.

We are grateful to participating landowners for allowing surveys and discussing possible measures. The Thames21 work, supported by Oxfordshire County Council, deliberately anonymises individual holdings; this site will follow the same principle unless a landowner specifically wishes to be identified.

What participation can mean

NFM features can require land, long-term maintenance and practical commitments. Changes in land use can affect farm output, so proposals need to respect the economics and day-to-day operation of each holding.

Where suitable, funding can help with leaky dams, bunds, ponds, swales and land-management measures. Temporary NFM storage is not automatically a summer irrigation reservoir, but complementary winter-water storage may offer wider resilience benefits where regulation, design and funding allow.

A nearby example

Learning from the Evenlode catchment.

The neighbouring Evenlode Catchment Partnership has used field storage bunds and leaky woody dams in rural Oxfordshire. During Storm Bert, a field bund above Milton-under-Wychwood held back about 1,700 m³ of floodwater, and the partnership reported that houses along Littlestock Brook did not flood on that occasion.

This is useful local evidence of NFM in practice, not a guarantee that identical measures will prevent flooding in Bloxham.
