Why this matters

Bloxham has flooded before. Storm Bert was the latest — and worst — episode.

Flooding is not new to Bloxham. The village has experienced serious flooding in the past, but Storm Bert in November 2024 was the latest and most severe episode in recent community experience. Between 22 and 24 November, intense rainfall caused serious flooding across the village. Community information indicates that around 65 properties were affected, although a precise total is not known. At least five vehicles were also badly affected.

One factor that made the November 2024 flooding worse was that many drains and gullies were blocked. But drainage maintenance alone cannot solve the problem: large volumes of water also run rapidly off the higher ground surrounding Bloxham.

Climate change adds urgency. UK climate projections point towards more frequent and intense weather extremes and heavier rainfall events, increasing pressure on rivers, surface-water routes and drainage systems. Bloxham therefore needs to strengthen its flood resilience now.

The figure of around 65 affected properties is a community estimate and is broader than the number formally recorded in official reporting.

Working together

Working with our landowners.

Much of Bloxham's potential Natural Flood Management work will need to take place on privately owned farmland. Its success therefore depends on voluntary cooperation with the people who own and farm the surrounding land.

Helping Bloxham — supporting our farmers

We are extremely grateful to the local farmers and landowners who are already engaging with the project, allowing land to be surveyed and discussing possible ways of slowing and storing runoff before it reaches Bloxham.

We also recognise the pressures facing farming. Landowners have businesses and livelihoods to protect, and decisions about changing the way land is used cannot be taken lightly — particularly after the exceptionally dry conditions experienced recently.

The aim is to work with landowners, not against them: identifying measures that can reduce flood risk for Bloxham while remaining practical for the farm, and wherever possible securing appropriate funding or financial support.

Not every piece of land will be suitable and not every landowner will choose to participate. The more opportunities that can be developed collaboratively across the catchment, however, the greater the potential to reduce and delay the peak flow reaching the village.

Can winter water become an asset?

Temporary Natural Flood Management storage is primarily designed to hold water during a storm and release it gradually afterwards. In suitable locations, separate or complementary farm water-storage measures may also allow winter rainfall to be retained for longer and used during dry periods.

That creates an important opportunity to explore two benefits together: reducing flood pressure on downstream neighbours during very wet periods, while improving farm resilience and water availability during dry summers.

Every farm is different. Water storage, abstraction and permanent reservoir proposals can have different regulatory and funding requirements from temporary NFM measures, so schemes need to be developed with appropriate professional advice.

A nearby example

The Evenlode shows NFM working.

The neighbouring Evenlode Catchment Partnership has delivered NFM in rural Oxfordshire for several years. On Littlestock Brook above Milton-under-Wychwood, measures include field storage bunds and leaky woody dams.

During Storm Bert, a field bund above Milton-under-Wychwood held back about 1,700 m³ of floodwater. The partnership reported that houses along Littlestock Brook did not flood on that occasion.
